Moments of Leaves - China Tourism News Network

📅December 27, 2025
🌐Translated: English
📄View Original

"Red flowers and green leaves" is a familiar aesthetic image. "The beauty of red flowers is enhanced by green leaves" is a widely recognized aesthetic principle. In classical poetry, whether it is the subtle contrast in "Layers of fresh green among the branches, with small buds hiding specks of red" or the striking juxtaposition in "Endless lotus leaves stretch to the sky, under the sun, lotus blooms glow uniquely red," this aesthetic concept is consistently emphasized. Red flowers are like a glamorous star singer at the center of the stage, while green leaves resemble the accompanying dancers swaying gracefully around them.

Has it always been this way? Perhaps not. Flowers are not necessarily red, nor are leaves always green. Moreover, different species of plants, or even the same species in different seasons, can vary greatly.

Has it always been beautiful this way? Again, perhaps not. I recall years ago, at the Beijing Ethnic Culture Palace Theater, watching Yang Liping at the peak of her career in a solo dance performance. It was not dance accompanying song, but song accompanying dance. The music and stage design have faded from memory, but her breathtaking peacock dance remains vivid in my mind. Of course, one could interpret this as the dancer being the flower and everything else the leaves.

Plants and trees take turns to shine throughout the seasons. This is the changing palette observed over years of living in Beijing. In the late autumn and early winter, it is not the flowers but the leaves that steal the show.

On a weekend afternoon, I took a stroll in Dongfeng Park. The ground was carpeted with large plane tree leaves—some green tinged with yellow, others yellow blushing red, and some red translucent enough to let light through. A single giant five-pointed leaf could cover an entire face. The endless carpet of fallen leaves made me hesitate, reluctant to tread further. My spouse crouched on the ground, carefully selecting leaves, surrounded by a kaleidoscope of colors as if standing in the midst of a painting. When she stood up, she held two large handfuls of colorful plane tree leaves, beaming like a child. She struck poses with her arms crossed, raised the leaves toward the setting sun, and finally tossed a big handful into the sky...

If there is any leaf more beautiful than the plane tree at this time of year, it is the ginkgo leaf. Indeed, unlike the desolation of willow leaves, the withering of peach leaves, the stiffness of poplar leaves, or even the mottled appearance of plane tree leaves, the dazzling ginkgo leaf is simply radiant. Hanging from the branches, they shimmer like gold; carpeting the ground, they gleam like a golden rug. Poets have described falling ginkgo leaves as "golden butterflies dancing gracefully" or "suddenly startling the world with their triumphant display." The natural spectacle they create is truly breathtaking and awe-inspiring.

Years ago, a young woman from southern China came to Beijing for the first time and was deeply moved by the golden ginkgo leaves of autumn. She crafted a delicate golden "leaf crown" and placed it on her braided hair, resembling a Greek goddess crowned with olive leaves. She collected many leaves, painted them into bookmarks, and gifted them to friends. To this day, I still keep that small yellow fan-shaped leaf with a panda leaning against a rock. Ginkgo leaves became her lasting memory of the capital's scenery.

However, after a few gusts of wind, the countdown begins again. As ginkgo leaves fade, the smoke trees turn red.

Xiangshan, located in western Beijing, is named after its rock formations that resemble incense burners. When I first arrived in Beijing, viewing the red leaves of Xiangshan was an annual aspiration. Despite being 20 kilometers away, Xiangshan remained the destination for our bike rides and hikes for many years.

After the first frost each year, as temperatures drop and the temperature difference widens, the over 100,000 smoke trees covering Xiangshan begin to change color, displaying shades of green, yellow, orange, and red. At their peak, they settle into a vibrant red, creating a magnificent landscape ranging from fiery red to deep purple.

An elderly writer once sighed, "These red leaves are like life itself—they grow in spring, flourish in summer, and only in autumn reveal their most brilliant colors."

During a visit to the National Art Museum of China, one collection piece stood out. The painting was a sea of red, with towering mountains dominating the center. In the foreground, red trees stood like silhouettes, framing white waterfalls, streams, and tiled houses, creating an immersive and almost audible scene. The painting directly draws from Mao Zedong's poetic imagery: "Mountains are all red, forests are dyed deep." It evokes a grand and majestic landscape, reflecting a longing for life and a celebration of existence, with a quiet vitality and upward spirit.

This is a famous work by the painter Li Keran from the 1960s. Although it explicitly depicts the landscapes of Sichuan and Guangdong, my first impression was that it was related to the red leaves of Xiangshan.

As expected.

I later learned that Li Keran created seven versions of "Mountains Are All Red, Forests Are Dyed Deep." The first two were painted in Conghua, Guangdong, in 1962 and 1963, while the remaining five were completed consecutively in 1964 at the Badachu studio near Xiangshan in Beijing.

Red leaves blaze like fire, igniting mountains far and wide. Li Keran, the renowned artist who advocated "plunging in with the greatest effort and emerging with the greatest courage," ultimately carved out a vibrant new artistic realm.

Looking out the window, clouds gather and disperse. Seasons alternate, flowers bloom, and leaves fall. Countless lives rise and fade in succession.

You support me, I help you. We come and go, we advance and retreat. You have your brilliance, I have my dignity.

In the end, perhaps it is not about who complements whom, but about shining in one’s own right. This may be the most important lesson of all.
